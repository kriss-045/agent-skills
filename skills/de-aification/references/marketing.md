# De-Aification: Marketing / Brand Voice Tells

Detect and remove AI-generation markers from social media posts, ad copy, brand messaging, product descriptions, and sales content.

**Sourcing note:** unlike writing / code / visual, this domain was NOT covered by a dedicated sourced study in the research. Everything below is OPINION — the core writing tells applied to marketing context plus platform conventions. Treat it as a practical checklist, not measured findings.

---

## General Pattern (Cross-Domain)

**Tell:** Marketing copy written by LLMs exhibits the same structural and linguistic patterns as other AI-generated text, with the addition of formulaic marketing structures.

The tells below are best detected by understanding the three core domains (writing, visual, code) and applying them to marketing context.

---

## Primary Marketing Tells (Apply Core Writing Rules)

### 1. Absence of Voice / Brand Personality
**Tell:** Generic, corporate tone that could apply to any brand. No distinctive word choices, no personality markers, no humor, no quirks.

**Fix:** Inject brand voice everywhere. If your brand is playful, use casual language and jokes. If serious, use domain-specific terminology and authority. Add personal observations and opinions. Every sentence should sound like your brand, not a template.

**Evidence:** OPINION (marketing practice)

---

### 2. Excessive Formal Language
**Tell:** Overuse of formal discourse markers (moreover, furthermore, consequently) in social media posts and ad copy where casual language is expected.

**Concrete example:**
"Furthermore, our product solves this problem. Moreover, it is cost-effective."
(Wrong for Twitter/Instagram; right for a white paper)

**Fix:** Match formality to platform. Twitter/TikTok: casual, punchy, short. LinkedIn: professional but not stiff. Website copy: varies by section. Remove formal connectors in social media entirely.

**Evidence:** OPINION (marketing platform conventions)

---

### 3. Lack of Specificity / Vagueness
**Tell:** General claims without numbers, examples, customer names, or timestamps.

**Concrete example:**
"Many customers report improvements" vs. "Netflix saved 40% on encoding costs after switching to H.265"

**Fix:** Always include specific numbers, customer names (with permission), dates, and measurable outcomes. Case studies should include before/after metrics. Testimonials should include role and company.

**Evidence:** OPINION (marketing effectiveness)

---

### 4. Overuse of Superlatives
**Tell:** Every claim is maximal: "best," "leading," "revolutionary," "game-changing," "industry-first."

**Fix:** Use superlatives sparingly, only for genuinely differentiated claims. For other features, use measured language: "faster," "more efficient," "simpler." Let data speak instead of hype.

**Evidence:** OPINION (marketing credibility)

---

### 5. Call-to-Action Repetition
**Tell:** CTA appears word-for-word multiple times in the same piece. "Sign up today" repeated in headline, subheadline, and button.

**Fix:** Vary CTAs. "Get started," "Start free trial," "Schedule a demo," "See pricing" — use different phrasings. CTA variation feels intentional and less formulaic.

**Evidence:** OPINION (copywriting best practice)

---

## Platform-Specific Tells

### Twitter / X Posts
**Tell:** Posts of uniform length, similar structure (problem-solution-CTA), and formal tone across the feed.

**Fix:** Vary post length and structure. Some threads, some single tweets, some replies. Vary tone: some playful, some serious, some conversational. Engage with other accounts, retweet, participate in conversations.

**Evidence:** OPINION (social media authenticity)

---

### LinkedIn Content
**Tell:** Articles with identical structure (intro-three-points-conclusion), recycled phrases ("Here's what I learned," "Looking forward to"), and no personal opinion.

**Fix:** Write unique articles. Some personal essays, some data-driven, some controversial. Vary structure and length. Include specific examples from your experience. Offer opinions, not just observations.

**Evidence:** OPINION (platform engagement patterns)

---

### Email Subject Lines
**Tell:** Subject lines use identical formulas ("5 Ways to X," "[ACTION] Do This," "Re: Your Question") repeated across emails.

**Fix:** Vary subject line structure. Some questions ("Do You Know?"), some statements ("This Changed Everything"), some curiosity gaps ("One Mistake..."). Mix all-caps emphasis with lowercase intrigue.

**Evidence:** OPINION (email marketing practice)

---

### Product Descriptions
**Tell:** Features listed in parallel structure with no variation. Every feature gets one sentence of the same length and grammatical structure.

**Fix:** Describe features in varied ways. Some as benefits (what you gain), some as technical specs (what it does), some as comparisons (vs. alternatives). Vary sentence length and complexity.

**Evidence:** OPINION (e-commerce best practice)

---

### Ad Copy
**Tell:** Ads use identical value propositions, same pain points, same CTA across different audience segments.

**Fix:** Segment your audience and write specific copy for each. Different messaging for different pain points. Different CTAs for different stages of the funnel (awareness → consideration → conversion).

**Evidence:** OPINION (advertising best practice)

---

## Language Patterns to Remove

### 1. Formulaic Openers
Remove: "In today's world," "As we all know," "It's no secret that," "Moving forward"
Replace with specific, contextual openings.

### 2. Buzzwords Without Substance
Remove: "synergize," "leverage," "disrupt," "ecosystem," "thought leadership"
Replace with specific, honest language.

### 3. Empty Modifiers
Remove: "really," "very," "quite," "somewhat," "arguably"
Replace with specific qualifiers: "3x faster," "20% increase," "fewer than 5 minutes"

### 4. Hedging in Confident Claims
Remove hedging from strong claims: "may," "might," "could," "seem," "suggest"
Use: "is," "will," "achieves," when claims are supported by evidence.

---

## Quick Checklist

Before posting:
- [ ] Voice distinct (would it fit another brand?)
- [ ] No superlatives unless differentiated by evidence
- [ ] Specific numbers, names, dates included
- [ ] CTAs vary across posts/emails/ads
- [ ] Formal language appropriate to platform (casual for social, formal for B2B)
- [ ] No repeated sentence structures within a piece
- [ ] Personal opinions or observations included (not just facts)
- [ ] Examples and case studies include metrics
- [ ] Unique subject lines (no formula repeated)
- [ ] Audience-specific messaging (not one-size-fits-all)

---

## Note

Marketing voice is deeply tied to brand identity. The tells above flag generic, templated copy. The best de-aification approach is to define your brand voice explicitly, then audit all marketing copy against it: "Does this sound like us?"
