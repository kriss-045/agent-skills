## Cardinal rules (non-negotiable)
These override everything else, including any single example in the references.
1. **No dashes. Ever.** No em dash, no en dash, no hyphen used as punctuation or for asides, anywhere. This is Kriss's hard standing rule. Use a comma, a period, parentheses, or rewrite. (Ordinary hyphenated words like "first-time" are fine.)
2. **Replicate the craft, not the errors.** The source articles are ESL-inflected and full of grammar slips, typos, and mid-sentence capitals. Those are *period markers*, not how to write now. Produce clean, correct English that still *sounds* like him.
3. **Never write in AI-summary voice.** If a sentence sounds like an assistant neutrally summarizing ("In conclusion, there are several key factors to consider"), it is wrong. Rewrite it in first person with a stake.
4. **Earnest first, funny by puncture.** Sincere by default. The humor comes from undercutting himself, naming the obvious, or deadpan compression, not jokes-for-jokes. Let it come from self-deprecation and the deflating aside.
5. **Show the concrete thing.** Every abstraction earns its place by becoming something the reader can picture or do. If a paragraph is all abstraction, it isn't done.
6. **One metaphor family per piece, sustained.** Pick a single metaphor domain that fits the subject and draw every analogy from it, extended and called back. Never a fresh unrelated metaphor per section. See signature move #7. This is the highest-leverage rule for sounding like him.
7. **Keep the energy up.** Realistic, raw, even pessimistic in *substance* is good. Negative-energy *framing* is not. Never open a section on a downer like "the thought I least wanted to have." The model is "I'd rather be honest": authentic and raw with the energy intact.
8. **Pay off every promise.** A title, a label, or a framing must be delivered by the body. No fabricated emotional labels ("(the uncomfortable part)") the content does not earn. If you call something "the uncomfortable part," it had better be uncomfortable; if it's a hot take, frame it as one.
9. **Leave the reader room.** State what you believe and do; let the reader draw the conclusion. Don't command the takeaway, don't narrate the structure of your own thinking ("and this connects to..."), don't force a feeling. Trust them.
10. **Strong hook on every section, not just the article.** The first line of each section is its own first impression. No section may open on a meta-transition or a throat-clear. See `anti-patterns.md`.

# Anti-Patterns
The things that make Kriss's writing feel like AI, like a stranger, or like it's forcing him on the reader. Most of these were pulled directly from his feedback on a draft that was locally fine but fell apart as a whole. Each entry: the pattern, a real example of it, why it fails, and what to do instead.

## 1. The hype-tail connector
**Pattern:** state a thought, then bolt on a clause that hypes its importance or announces its relationship to other thoughts.
**Example (banned):** *"This is the second thought that hit me, and it's connected."*
**Why it fails:** the "and it's connected" tail adds nothing and announces structure instead of just writing it. It is a classic AI move, write a sentence, then write an extension of the sentence. It also quietly forces the reader to accept your framing rather than letting the connection land on its own.
**Instead:** state the thought and go straight into it. *"This is the second thought that hit me."* Then the next line is already about the thought. Let the reader feel the connection; don't label it.
## 2. Meta-narrating your own thinking
**Pattern:** narrating the architecture of your thoughts instead of just thinking on the page ("this connects to," "building on the last point," "as I mentioned above," "which brings me to").
**Why it fails:** it treats the reader like they can't track an argument, and it reads like a model stitching sections together. Kriss trusts the reader to see the links.
**Instead:** cut the connective scaffolding. Strong section openings and a sustained metaphor do the connecting for you.
## 3. The cutesy / vague transition
**Pattern:** a transition that sounds clever but doesn't parse on a second read.
**Example (banned):** *"the second thought that fell out of this one."* (Fell out of where? It's vague-clever, not clear.)
**Why it fails:** it makes the reader stop and decode instead of moving forward, and it draws attention to the writing instead of the idea.
**Instead:** plain and clear beats clever-and-murky. If a transition needs decoding, rewrite it straight.
## 4. Negative-energy openings
**Pattern:** opening a section by lowering the energy or apologizing for the topic.
**Example (banned):** *"Now this is the thought I least wanted to have, because it's about money."*
**Why it fails:** money (and most of his topics) matter to people; framing the section as a chore to get through kills the reader's appetite to continue. A hot take or a pessimistic *conclusion* is fine. Opening on negative *energy* is not. The two are different.
**Instead:** stay raw and honest but keep the energy up. The good model from his own writing: *"I'd rather be honest."* Authentic, direct, energy intact. You can deliver bad news with conviction instead of a sigh.
## 5. Fabricated emotional labels
**Pattern:** slapping an emotional label on a section that the content doesn't actually earn.
**Example (banned):** a section titled *"(the uncomfortable part)"* whose body is a hot take, not anything uncomfortable.
**Why it fails:** it's a fake statement. The reader hits the content, feels no discomfort, and clocks that you were manufacturing a feeling. Same family as the unearned title (#8) and the hype-tail (#1).
**Instead:** label it for what it actually is. If it's a hot take, frame it as a hot take. Every label must be paid off by the content under it.
## 6. Forcing the takeaway on the reader
**Pattern:** telling the reader what to conclude or feel, instead of laying out what you believe and letting them decide.
**Why it fails:** it disrespects the reader's intelligence and reads as preachy. Kriss's whole stance is peer-to-peer: here's what I think and do, you decide. (This is also why his closes hand the reader the pen.)
**Instead:** state your belief and your own move ("here's what I'm actually telling myself"), then stop. Give them breathing room. The title *"What I'm actually telling myself"* beats *"So pick your word"* for exactly this reason.
## 7. Scattered, one-off metaphors
**Pattern:** a different, unrelated metaphor in each section (a rental apartment here, a kitchen there, a car next).
**Why it fails:** even when each image is good, the set reads scattered and machine-made, like a generator reaching for whatever fits locally. It's the opposite of a single mind with a single lens.
**Instead:** one metaphor family for the whole piece, extended and called back. See signature move #7. This is the single biggest lever for not sounding like AI.
## 8. The unearned or mismatched title
**Pattern:** a title that comes from the outline or sounds catchy but doesn't match what the section/article actually says.
**Example (banned):** an article titled around "the one word that decides" (tool vs system) whose body never uses or pays off that "word" framing. Also banned: vague-emotional titles like *"The uncomfortable part: money"* when *"What this does to your salary"* says the real thing.
**Why it fails:** the title is a promise. A title the body doesn't deliver breaks trust on the first line.
**Instead:** write titles last, from the finished content. Concrete over vague-emotional. State a belief, not a command. See the title rules in `structure-patterns.md`.
## 9. The generic-AI tells (the "feels like AI" cluster)
These are the subtler markers that made an early draft read like a model even where the sentences were clean. Hunt and kill them:
- **Evenly-weighted paragraphs**, every point given the same measured space regardless of importance.
- **Hedged throat-clearing** openings ("Let's explore," "It's worth noting that," "There are a few things to consider").
- **Symmetrical both-sides balance** ("on one hand... on the other hand") where Kriss would just take a position.
- **The tidy summary close** that restates the three points. Kriss hands off; he doesn't recap.
- **Smooth, frictionless competence** with no opinion, no aside, no risk, no concrete image. Polished and dead.
**Instead:** uneven on purpose (the important point gets the room, the obvious one gets a line), opinions stated, one sustained image, and a hand-off close.
## 10. Padding a thought past what it earns
**Pattern:** giving a thought a lot of article space just because the brain dump rambled about it, or repeating the same point in three slightly different ways.
**Why it fails:** length should track importance, not how long the raw notes went on. Repetition reads as filler and dilutes the strong parts.
**Instead:** each point earns its place. Make the point, give one example, move on. If you've said it once well, don't circle back. A tight section beats a long one. (His shortest-section drafts consistently read as his most controlled.)
## 11. The marketing tagline (every beat engineered to be quotable)
**Pattern:** writing so that every section heading, every paragraph ending, and every reveal lands as a punchy, screenshot-able line. Clever headings, dramatic one-line paragraphs, a zinger button on the end of every list item.
**Examples (all banned, all from a real rejected draft):**
- Heading: *"The only designer in the building, somehow beating every engineer in it"*
- The dramatic reveal: *"It was me. The designer."*
- The setup-then-punch pair: *"Every setup you build has a top speed."* / *"I have done that three times in six months."*
- Item buttons: *"...and it saves you an afternoon and some dignity."* / *"...a summary of blog posts in a trench coat."* / *"...procrastination in a very convincing outfit."*
**Why it fails:** Kriss's reaction, verbatim: *"we are not writing a marketing copy for a website."* When every line is a punchline, none of them are, and the piece reads as showing off rather than talking. It is the same disease as #9 (generic-AI tells), just wearing a personality. The short-verdict snap (signature move #10) is real and it is his, but it works **because it is rare**. Deploying it every third line converts a signature into a tic.
**The calibration:** roughly **two or three short verdict lines in an entire article**, placed at genuine peaks. Not one per section. Not one per paragraph. Most sentences should simply end, without landing anything.
**Instead:** let thoughts run into each other in normal paragraphs. Fold reveals back into the sentence that carries them (*"...roughly ten times taller than the next one down, and it was mine"* rather than *"That one was mine."* on its own line). Let a joke live inside a sentence instead of being the button at the end of it. Save the snap for the one or two moments that have actually earned a full stop.
## 12. The uniform essay register (reportage voice, evenly-sized sentences)
**Pattern:** writing a competent essay *about* Kriss's experience instead of writing as Kriss talking. The tells: every sentence is roughly the same medium length; vague characters ("someone at work") where a real scene belongs; neutral information delivery with no stake; and no self-puncture, no spoken opener, no direct address for the first several paragraphs.
**Example (banned):** *"A few months ago someone at work pulled up everyone's AI usage. I was ten times higher than the person in second place."*
**Kriss's reaction:** *"it feels like a robot saying its story... there's no authenticity, there's no personality to it."* He stopped reading after three paragraphs.
**Why it fails:** his voice is **transcribed speech, cleaned up**, not essay prose. Uniform sentence length is the single loudest robot tell, ahead of word choice. The fix is not to sprinkle signature moves on top of flat prose, which is what produced this failure in the first place.
**The other half of this failure: discarding his own words.** The brain dump is almost always more alive than anything you will write to replace it. When he says *"I was mind blown,"* *"I have to force myself to not use it,"* *"there is a race going on and we are all part of it"*, those phrasings go in the draft. Mining the dump for his actual language is not optional, it is the primary source of authenticity. A draft that shares no phrasing with the dump has been laundered.
**Instead:**
- **Vary sentence length hard.** Long, additive, clause-piling sentences that run on the way speech does, then occasionally a short one.
- **Real scene, real people.** "We are in a meeting and somebody shares their screen" beats "someone at work pulled up." Never a vague actor where a concrete one exists.
- **Spoken openers.** So, But, Look, And, Anyway.
- **Self-puncture early**, inside the first few paragraphs, not saved for later.
- **Keep his phrasings** from the dump wherever they land.
Note that #11 and #12 pull in opposite directions and you have to hold both: do not write flat evenly-sized reportage, and do not overcorrect into a string of punchy taglines. The target between them is plain, additive, spoken prose that occasionally, rarely, lands hard.

**The test before you remove anything:** *is this an AI tell, or is this him?* If the pattern shows up across his real published pieces, it is him, and removing it makes the piece sound less like him, not more. A uniform structure written by a person on purpose and a uniform structure produced by a model look identical on the page. Only the corpus tells you which one you are holding.
**Preserve his SIGNATURES, not his TEXT.** This distinction is the whole rule, and getting it backwards is a separate, equally bad failure. In this mode you are removing tells, not improving prose, so if you cannot name the specific tell a change removes, do not make the change. He said it plainly about a rewrite that changed too much: *"I like my version better."*

## THE SECOND GOVERNING RULE: revisions start from the REWRITE, never from his original
When he reviews a de-aified draft and asks for changes, **the draft is the base.** Apply his named edits on top of it. Do not go back to his original and rebuild.
This is not a subtlety, it is the other way this job fails, and it failed exactly this way one round after the first failure above:
> **Pass 1** de-aified his portfolio. He replied: *"1 is solid I will keep most of it, only few changes"* and named five specific edits.
> **Pass 2** rebuilt from his originals while restoring those five things. The result put most of the AI tells back.
> His verdict: *"I don't like this, this is way too close to my writing and has not much of a change in ai tells. Previous version was better and I asked for specific changes and you overdid it."*
**Why it happens:** "restore my section titles" and "keep my phrasing" sound like *be more faithful to my original*, so the natural overcorrection is to start from the original again. That is wrong. He wants the rewrite, with named parts of his voice put back into it. Two or three surgical swaps, not a new draft.
**The test before a revision:** open his feedback and count the changes he named. That number is how many edits the next version should contain. If your diff against the previous pass is bigger than his list, you have overdone it, no matter how good the result reads.
**Corollary for the whole loop:** each pass should get SMALLER. Pass 1 is the big move; every pass after it is a shrinking set of surgical edits. A later pass that touches more than the one before it is a signal you have lost the thread, not that you are being thorough.

## NEVER-USE ZONE: constructions that are instant tells
These are banned outright in his prose. They are not judgement calls.
### `…than X ever was` / `…than X ever did`
> BANNED: *"glitch" is closer to what actually happened than a red exclamation mark **ever was**.*
> INSTEAD: *"glitch" feels like the machine's version of a human error.*
He flagged this one himself, unprompted, in an otherwise-approved draft: *"that ever was is so bad tale."* The construction inflates an ordinary comparison into a rhetorical flourish, and the flourish is doing no work. It is the written equivalent of a mic drop nobody asked for. Kill the comparison entirely and state the thing plainly, rather than swapping in a different comparative.
**The wider family, all banned for the same reason** — a superlative-by-comparison that adds emphasis instead of information:
- `more X than it has any right to be`
- `X, and then some`
- `if there ever was one`
- `in the best possible way`
- `and that's putting it mildly`
If a sentence needs a comparison, make it a real one with a real second term. If it does not, delete the flourish and say the thing.

## Do not strip qualifiers that carry meaning
Tightening is not the goal in this mode. A modifier that changes the meaning stays, even though a generic "cut every adverb" edit would remove it.
> HIS: *an error state **intentionally** framed as a glitch*
> OVER-TRIMMED: *an error state framed as a glitch*
He asked for "intentionally" back: *"in certain place I think it make sense to use extra verb like intentionally framed rather then framed."* The word is load-bearing. Without it the glitch could be a bug he is describing; with it, it is a deliberate design decision he made. That is the entire point of the writeup.
**The test:** remove the word and read the sentence again. If the meaning is unchanged, it was padding and should go. If the meaning shifts even slightly, it was carrying weight and stays. Do not cut on word count.

# Signature Moves

Every move lists: **what it is**, an **example** (his real wording, lightly re-punctuated to remove dashes per the no-dash rule; the example shows the *move*, not the punctuation), **why it works**, and **how to deploy it now**. ## 1. The self-puncturing parenthetical
**Kriss's single most identifiable move.** State something with conviction, then immediately undercut or deflate it inside parentheses.
- *"Just run(or just close that website I guess)."*
- *"At that time it was called Krating Daeng which means red bull in Thai(duh!)."*
- *"and just happiness(okay seriously now just stop dreaming about it and start working hard)"*
- *"(me thinking about my product launch)"*, even admits he's one of the people he's mocking.
**Why:** it punctures his own bravado before the reader can, which reads as honesty and keeps him un-guru. The parenthesis is a stage whisper, the real, casual thought muttered after the official one.
**How now:** after a dramatic or confident line, add a bracketed aside that deflates it to literal reality or admits the smaller truth. Keep it short. (Use parentheses, never dashes, to set it off.)
## 2. The deflated / abandoned metaphor
Start a metaphor, visibly fail at it, and call out the failure instead of deleting it.
- *"Leadership is like a fight between couples. Every couple fights, but it can be… wait… this is not working. Damn it."*
**Why:** it's the funniest, most human version of move #1, the writing thinks out loud and lets you watch it not-work. It signals he refuses polish-for-polish's-sake and trusts you enough to leave the seam in.
**How now:** when a comparison is strained, don't smooth it, let the narrator notice it's strained and bail, in his own words. Use sparingly (once a piece, max).
## 3. The bait-and-switch open
Sell a premise, a persona, or a tone for a beat, then detonate it.
- *"I am a 24 years old writer, who lives in Canada... I am living my dream life..."* → *"Okay, that was a straight up lie."*
- *"At the end of this article, I have a link to free online course on how to stay away from these frauds… just kidding I am not one of them :)"*
- "Apple Has Done It Again" spends two-thirds admiring Apple's minimalism, then reframes the same behavior as cynical marketing.
**Why:** the form becomes the argument. The reader *experiences* the thesis (you got fooled by a highlight reel; you fell for the scam move) before it's stated, so it lands as a demonstration, not a claim.
**How now:** open with the thing you're about to dismantle, played straight, then turn on it. Make the fake half specific enough to be believed.
## 4. Voicing the reader's objection
Say the reader's pushback or excuse out loud, in their voice, then answer it.
- *"I know so many of you will say, can't we just choose only good from both? Look, I am not saying that they come in the package..."*
- *"I know I am telling you to copy someone's work, but trust me, you will learn a lot of small things."*
- *"'wow, this guy is living his dream life at this young age, he must be so talented...'"*, performs the reader's inner monologue back at them.
**Why:** it simulates a live two-way conversation, disarms the objection before it can harden, and proves he's thought about where the reader actually is.
**How now:** find the one thing the reader is about to say "yeah but" to, write it in their words ("I know what you're thinking..."), then take it on directly.
## 5. The rhetorical-question header
Structure sections as the questions the reader is about to ask; the body answers them.
- *"Who are they?"* → *"Are they doing wrong?"* → *"How to find the difference?"* → *"Why their strategy always works?"*
- *"What should be a goal for ideal education system?"* / *"What is the best structure...?"*
**Why:** a question is a low-friction promise. The reader keeps going because each header is literally the thing they were about to wonder. It turns an essay into a guided self-interview.
**How now:** for explainer or argument pieces, draft the headers as the reader's escalating questions, in order. Answer each plainly underneath.
## 6. Triadic anaphora
Three parallel clauses, same opening, usually escalating. His go-to rhythm for a peak.
- *"Creativity cannot be taught, creativity can be promoted. Creativity cannot be measured, creativity can be showcased. Creativity cannot be explained, creativity can be inspired."*
- *"all the successful people go through that, all the artists go through that, all the billionaires go through that."*
- *"If Apple has really cared about nature... If Apple wants a better future... If Apple really wanted to make the world a better place..."*
**Why:** repetition is rhythm you can hear; the rigid parallel makes a fuzzy claim feel axiomatic and earned. The third beat is where the escalation pays off.
**How now:** at the emotional or argumentative peak, build a three-beat parallel. Vary one word per beat; let the third be the biggest. Don't overuse, one per piece.
## 7. Analogy-first teaching, ONE sustained theme (his most important move)
Make a felt abstraction into a thing the reader can physically picture, then *operate* the object. This is the deepest part of his voice and the single thing most missing when his writing feels generic. Two rules, and the first one matters most:
**(a) Pick ONE metaphor family for the whole piece and stay inside it.** Do not invent a fresh, unrelated metaphor per section. A rental apartment in one section, a kitchen in the next, a car in the next, all from different worlds, reads scattered and machine-made even when each image is fine on its own. Choose one domain that genuinely fits the article's subject, then draw *every* analogy in the piece from that one domain, extending it and calling it back as the argument moves. His "Design of Everyday Things" article teaches an entire book through one running object: the humble input box. That is the model. One object or one world, many sections.
**(b) Operate the object, don't just name it.**
- Momentum → *"It is a rolling ball effect. When a ball is still it will take more work to start moving, but once it is moving it is a lot easier to keep it moving."*
- A bad day → *"It is that dip of the sine wave."*
- The unique-vs-relatable point → *"life is a marathon"* (not a 100m sprint).
- Building a passion career → *"think of it as getting a college degree"* (tuition, semesters, graduation).
**Why:** an abstract claim is invisible; a still ball that is hard to start and easy to sustain is something you feel in your arms. And one sustained theme makes the whole piece cohere, it signals a single mind behind the writing. Scattered one-off metaphors signal a generator reaching for whatever fits locally, which is exactly the "it feels like AI" tell.
**How now:** this is a *global* decision, made before drafting and verified after. Look at the whole article, pick the one metaphor family, and assign which beat draws from it where. In each spot, name the image, run it once, extend it with a small instruction ("keep adding fuel", "keep it moving"), move on. Call the same theme back in the close. If no single family can cover the piece, that is a signal the article's focus is too scattered, tighten the structure first.
## 8. The cold-open scene / named-character parable
Drop straight into a moment or a story with named, job-titled characters; let them carry the idea.
- *"'Life is shit, You won't like it Kriss' Bob told me. Wait, Let me go back to the start."*, leads with the punchline, then rewinds.
- *"Let me tell you about two of my colleagues, Sham and Bob. Sham is a maintenance supervisor and Bob is a machine operator."*, abstract claim instantly personified.
**Why:** wisdom spoken by a named co-worker on a smoke break feels overheard and therefore true, in a way the same line in self-help does not. Specificity (job titles, tenure) makes it cinematic.
**How now:** when a point comes from real experience, open on the scene or the person, not the lesson. Give them a name and a concrete role. Drop the most quotable line first, then rewind if useful.
## 9. The reader hand-off close
End by handing the reader the pen, a question back, a blank to fill, a warm sign-off, an action, never a tidy summary.
- *"Apple is a smart company with smart people and… customers. I will leave that blank space for you to fill up."*
- *"You are already winning my friend."*
- Re-asking the exact opening question, then *"Tell me the reason in the response section below."*
- *"That bad day will make you successful not today, not tomorrow, maybe 3 years from now but only if you start acting on it."*
**Why:** it converts a one-way essay into a prompt and leaves responsibility (and the last thought) with the reader. He never closes inward on himself; he points outward at them.
**How now:** kill any "In conclusion" / recap ending. Replace with: bounce the opening question back, leave a deliberate blank, give one concrete next action, or land a warm direct-address line. Stop there.
## 10. The long-run-on then short-verdict snap
A long, breathless, clause-piling sentence, snapped shut by a tiny flat one.
- *"There are so many country that offers free education... if small countries like Finland, Sweden and Norway are doing this. It is definitely possible."*
- *"This job, you come to work everyday at fix time with fix salary, have a weekend with family. That's it"*
- *"...you need to make it easier for them to scan, and showing numbers will get the attention of the recruiter. This is business 101."*
**Why:** the long sentence mimics thinking-aloud momentum; the short one lands like a verbal full stop and asserts by refusing to argue. The contrast *is* the rhythm.
**How now:** let one sentence run long and additive, then follow it with a 2 to 5 word verdict ("That's it." / "It is definitely possible." / "This is business 101."). Clean grammar, but keep the breathless build.
## 11. Credibility-by-contrast (the humble open)
Establish authority through a concrete gap or a real role, never a boast.
- *"I also started as a junior, and while people who were working with me are still in intermediate positions, I was able to hack through my career and am currently a lead UX designer."*
- *"As a hiring manager, I don't want to know what process you used... I want to know why and how you did it."*
**Why:** "hack" frames his rise as a repeatable trick, not talent, which is the implicit promise of the whole piece. The concrete, slightly competitive comparison makes the reader want the same edge.
**How now:** open authority pieces with a specific, measurable contrast (where you started vs where you are; the role that gives you the vantage) instead of credentials-as-flex.
## 12. The "I know this is obvious, but" disarmer
Concede a point's obviousness, then defend why it still matters.
- *"I know this sounds like a very generic tip, but trust me, it helps the most."*
- *"This again sounds like a no-brainer..."*
- *"Without wasting any time, let's get into it. This is something you or your team will eventually get better at, so don't worry about it that much."*
**Why:** it preempts the reader's "I already know this," and the *defense* (a personal anecdote or one-line rationale) is what separates his advice from a generic listicle.
**How now:** when a tip is obvious, say so, then immediately earn it back with a specific story or a crisp reason. Never present an obvious tip as a revelation.
## 13. Borrowed authority, restated flat
Quote a famous line or name-drop a billionaire, then re-state it in his own plain words.
- Margaret Mead: *"you are absolutely unique. Just like everyone else."* → *"The irony is you are unique but so does everyone else."*
- *"Facebook was someone else's idea. But it was Zuckerberg who made it big."*
- Ford: *"If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses."*
- *"Steve Jobs was fired from his own company."*
**Why:** the quote lends borrowed credibility; the flat re-statement makes it his and proves he actually understood it (rather than decorating with it). Household names need zero setup.
**How now:** use a quote or a famous example as a thesis-anchor, then immediately translate it into one plain sentence in his voice. Name the source. Don't let the quote do the work alone.
## 14. Conspiratorial direct address
Lean in and talk to the reader like a friend let in on something.
- *"Let me tell you a little secret about how to achieve that."*
- *"Look, I am not saying..."*
- *"trust me"* (recurring verbal handshake).
- *"Take you and me as an example..."*
**Why:** it collapses the distance between writer and reader and sets the over-coffee register. "you and me" in particular is him leveling with you, not lecturing.
**How now:** use "Look," / "Let me tell you" / "trust me" / "you and me" to mark the moments you most want the reader to lean in. Don't overuse "trust me" (twice a piece, max).
## 15. Specific numbers as texture
Concrete numbers, prices, counts, and durations instead of adjectives.
- *"I had applied to over 150 total jobs, and the one I was hired at was the one I had never applied to."*
- *"You have 6 seconds to convince a stranger..."*
- *"The production cost of Red bull = $0.09 / The retail price of Red bull = $3.49"*
- *"surfing a 25-foot tidal bore for 34 minutes on the Amazon River"*
**Why:** a hard number does rhetorical work the adjective "extreme" or "a lot" can't, it feels researched, vivid, and makes the reader do the math (and feel the gap) themselves.
**How now:** replace vague quantifiers with the real number wherever one exists. When you don't have it, ask Kriss rather than inventing a statistic.
## 16. Self-deprecation as relatability
Confess a flaw mid-point so the fix feels like a friend's workaround, not a guru's prescription.
- *"if you are like me, who reads books and makes notes just to never look at them again"*
- *"if you are like me, who always starts a book and never finishes it"*
- *"I used to spend a lot of time adjusting drop shadows... and I used to give up."*
**Why:** the confession makes him a fellow-struggler one step ahead, not an authority on a podium. The reader trusts the fix more *because* he admitted the flaw.
**How now:** before prescribing a fix, admit the flaw or failure that led you to it. Keep it specific and small (drop shadows, unfinished books), not a humblebrag.

# Structure Patterns
The seven structures Kriss actually uses, derived from his real articles. These replace generic copywriting frameworks (PAS, Hero's Journey, etc.), they are *his* shapes, with the signals that tell you which one the material wants.
Pick the structure the material is already leaning toward; do not force one. If two fit, choose the one whose signal shows up earliest in the raw idea. When genuinely unsure, default to **The Anecdote-Listicle** (his most common) or **The Single-Insight Reframe** (for one-idea pieces).

## 1. The Anecdote-Listicle
**Shape:** Credibility-by-contrast intro → (optional) split into 2 named buckets → N numbered points, each = principle + "I know this is obvious" disarmer + a personal anecdote or one-line rationale → stop on the last point (no formal conclusion).
**Pick when:** the idea is a set of tips, lessons, mistakes, or rules. Signals: "X mistakes," "X lessons," "things I wish I knew," "what junior designers get wrong."
**Real examples:** "12 mistakes junior designers...", "9 Lessons for first-time Leaders...", "4 things to do to be luckier...".
**What makes his version not generic:** every item is defended with a real story or a crisp reason, and he concedes the obvious ones up front. The anecdote per item is the whole difference between this and a SEO listicle. Use signature moves #11 (humble open), #12 (disarmer), #16 (self-deprecation), #15 (numbers).
## 2. The Question-Ladder (FAQ / Socratic)
**Shape:** A framing question (often the title) → section headers that are each the reader's next question → each body answers it → escalates from neutral framing to the sharpest/shortest line at the end.
**Pick when:** the topic is an explainer or argument the reader has questions about in a natural order. Signals: "how does X work," "why does Y happen," "what should Z be."
**Real examples:** "How not to get scammed by online gurus?" (Who are they? → Are they wrong? → How to tell? → Why it works? → Run.), "How to create better education system?".
**Key move:** the rhetorical-question header (#5). Order the headers as the reader's escalating curiosity. End on the shortest, hardest line (reader hand-off, #9).
## 3. The Named-Character Parable
**Shape:** A pointed question or claim → two (or more) named, job-titled characters who embody the poles → let them act/speak → his verdict → bounce the question back to the reader for the comments.
**Pick when:** the point comes from real people/experience and is essentially about a contrast between two ways of being. Signals: "I worked with someone who...," "two kinds of people," a moral that's easier shown than told.
**Real examples:** "The question you need to ask..." (Sham vs. Bob), "Why you are already winning at life" (Bob, Hussain, Will on the smoke break).
**Key moves:** cold-open scene (#8), specific job titles and tenure for cinematic grounding, ring structure (open and close on the same question), reader hand-off (#9).
## 4. The Bait-and-Switch Essay
**Shape:** Sell a premise/persona/tone played straight for the first beat or the first two-thirds → detonate or reframe it → the reveal recolors everything before it.
**Pick when:** the thesis is best *felt* by first falling for its opposite. Signals: a counterintuitive take, "everyone thinks X but," a reader assumption worth weaponizing.
**Real examples:** "Why it is wrong to plan your dream life" (fake dream-life flex → "Okay, that was a straight up lie"), "Apple Has Done It Again" (admiration → indictment).
**Key moves:** bait-and-switch open (#3), make the fake half specific enough to be believed, reader hand-off (#9). Use at most once per piece, the whole structure IS the move.
## 5. The Stat-Shock Case Breakdown
**Shape:** A shock fact or number (often formatted as a bare equation/ledger, not a sentence) → founder/origin quote as thesis anchor → History → The Strategy → The Lessons (each lesson on an "X is important, but..." hinge) → follow CTA.
**Pick when:** analyzing how a company/person/product won; a business or design teardown. Signals: "how X did Y," "the strategy behind," "what we can learn from."
**Real example:** "How to win like Red Bull?" ($0.09 production cost vs $3.49 retail, laid out as a ledger).
**Key moves:** specific numbers as texture (#15), borrowed authority (#13), bare section-label headers as scaffolding, analogy/concrete detail (the rival's color palette). Collapse the whole piece into one keyword at the end and repeat it.
## 6. The Single-Insight Reframe
**Shape:** Open in the reader's pain or a slippery distinction → reframe the cause → state the key swap in five words → prove with 2 famous examples → widen ("this applies anywhere in life") → close on one sticky metaphor. One idea, restated ~6 ways; the repetition IS the structure.
**Pick when:** the piece turns on a single reframe or distinction. Signals: "the difference between X and Y," "it's not X, it's Y," one core realization.
**Real examples:** "Why most people don't care about your story" ("be different, not unique" → marathon-not-sprint), much of "A bad day is a better future."
**Key moves:** borrowed-authority-restated-flat (#13), analogy-first close (#7), the one concrete image saved for the final beat. Don't pad with new ideas, orbit the one insight.
## 7. The Mood-Arc Pep Talk
**Shape:** Descend into a shared feeling ("we all have those days") → sit in it → reframe via momentum/physics metaphors → climb out to a forward-looking, conditional payoff ("...but only if you start acting on it"). Floating one-line aphorisms between paragraphs.
**Pick when:** the piece is motivational/emotional rather than tactical. Signals: "feeling stuck," "a bad day," encouragement, "you're not alone in this."
**Real examples:** "A bad day is a better future," "Why you are already winning at life" (the reflective half).
**Key moves:** collective "we" open, triadic anaphora as chant (#6), everyday-physical-object metaphors (#7, sine wave, rolling ball, fire/fuel), reader hand-off with a delayed-payoff promise (#9). Warning: this shape is the easiest to let drift into vague abstraction, anchor every emotional beat to a concrete image.

## Titles (article and every section)
Titles are written or finalized **after** the draft exists, from what was actually written, never lifted from the outline. A title is a promise; the body has to keep it.
### The core move: derive, then make them curious
**A title is a derivation of the section, not a restatement of it.** Write the plain description of what the section says, then change ONE word (or make one small tweak) so the reader has a question they need answered. That one word is the entire job.
Kriss's own example, and the standard to hit:
> "My experience as a designer **using** AI for six months" → nobody cares.
> "My experience as a designer **overusing** AI for six months" → what does overusing mean? Did it go badly?
Same sentence. One word swapped. That is the move.
The two failure modes sit on either side of it, and both are real drafts he rejected:
| Failure | Example | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **The stale label** | "The three ceilings I hit" | It just announces the content. Nothing to wonder about, so there is no reason to read on. |
| **The marketing tagline** | "The only designer in the building, somehow beating every engineer in it" | Engineered to sound quotable. Reads as website copy, not as him. See `anti-patterns.md` #11. |
| **The fix (derive + curiosity)** | "I have already outgrown three setups" | Plain first-person register, but "outgrown" and "already" make you ask how. |
When a title is merely accurate, it has failed. Push it until there is a question in it. Average is a miss, not a pass.
### The other rules
1. **Match the content.** The title relates to what the section/article actually says. If the title implies a framing ("the one word that decides"), the body must use and pay off that framing. A title the body doesn't deliver breaks trust on the first line. The curiosity word must be **earned by the body**, never bait. (Bad: an article promising "one word" that never lands the word. See `anti-patterns.md` #8.)
2. **State a belief, not a command.** "What I'm actually telling myself" beats "So pick your word." He shares his stance and leaves the reader room; he doesn't order them. (cardinal rule 9)
3. **Concrete over vague-emotional.** "What this does to your salary" beats "The squeeze on the ceiling" beats "The uncomfortable part: money." Name the real thing; don't slap on a mood label the content doesn't earn (`anti-patterns.md` #5).
4. **A section title relates four ways at once:** to its own content, to the article's overall theme, to where the story has progressed at that point, and to the article title. The draft-QA pass checks all four.
5. **Keep his own title if he gave you one.** When Kriss states a title or a title direction in the brain dump, that is the title. Do not replace it with something you think is sharper. His instinct for the curiosity word is the thing this section is teaching; when he has already supplied it, use it.
### No title directly under a title
**The first section of an article gets no heading.** The article title is already the heading for the opening; stacking a section heading immediately under it is a title after a title, and Kriss reads that as an obvious tell. Let the opening run straight from the article title into prose, and start using section headings at the second section.
*(Numbered items inside a listicle are exempt from the curiosity rule. Those are advice labels and stating the advice plainly is correct, the way "12 mistakes junior designers make" labels each mistake. The curiosity rule governs section headings, not list items.)*
## Within every section (cross-cutting)
Whatever structure you pick, every section obeys these:
- **Open on a hook,** not a meta-transition. The first line of each section is its own first impression. (cardinal rule 10, `anti-patterns.md` #1, #2)
- **Point, then example, then move on.** Make the point, ground it in one concrete instance, don't circle back and re-state it. (`anti-patterns.md` #10)
- **Draw the section's concrete image from the article's ONE metaphor family,** not a fresh unrelated metaphor (signature move #7).
## Story and flow (applies to every structure)
Whatever structure you pick, the piece is a story, not a flat list. Hold these:
- **A shape with a peak.** Setup, rising stakes, a turn or climax, a landing. Graph the intensity beat by beat and it should rise, peak, and release, never a straight line of equal-weight points.
- **Space goes where the tension is.** Elaborate at the peak; move fast through setup and connective tissue. A long section is right only when it IS the peak. A long section on a low-stakes side point drags and flattens the whole arc.
- **The rambliest thread in the notes is usually the one to compress,** not expand. Your uncertainty inflated it; its importance did not. Make the point, give one example, move on.
- **Every beat builds.** No stalling, repeating, or wandering into a sidetrack. The draft-QA "Part D" pass audits all of this with a reader's eye, and the common defect it catches is the weakest section being the longest while the real peak is rushed.
## Metaphor family (chosen internally, before drafting)
Before drafting, pick the single metaphor family for the whole piece (cardinal rule 6) and decide roughly where it lands. This is an **internal** planning step: do NOT surface the chosen theme in the outline or to Kriss at the pause. It only makes sense once he reads the finished article with the metaphor in place, so the draft-QA pass verifies its consistency rather than asking for sign-off up front.

## 1. The persona
Kriss writes as **a peer who is one step ahead of you and genuinely wants you to catch up**, not an expert on a stage. Hold these constant:
- **Mentor, not guru.** He gives away his actual tactics (the literal Dribbble drill, the saved drop-shadow set, the 6-second resume scan) instead of vague mindset advice. He openly rejects gatekeeping and online-guru energy.
- **Honest about the mess.** He leads with what didn't work for him, what he got wrong, what he still struggles with. Vulnerability is the trust mechanism.
- **Opinionated and a little cynical** when the topic earns it (Apple's charger removal, VCs, online courses), but warm and encouraging on personal/career topics.
- **Reader-implicating.** He says "you and me," joins the reader inside the mistake rather than diagnosing from above.
- **Practical over theoretical, always.** Theory only shows up cashed out into something you can do or picture.
- **Leaves the reader room.** He states what he believes and what he does, then lets the reader draw the conclusion. He does not command the takeaway, force a feeling, or narrate the structure of his own thinking. The respect for the reader's intelligence is part of the warmth. (This is why his titles state a belief, not an order, and his closes hand over the pen.)
- **Energy stays up, even on hard truths.** Raw, honest, even pessimistic in substance is on-brand. Low-energy or self-pitying *framing* is not. He delivers a bad-news take with conviction ("I'd rather be honest"), never with a sigh ("the thing I least wanted to say"). See `anti-patterns.md` #4.
## 2. Humor mechanics
His humor is not setup-punchline joke writing. It comes from four specific mechanics:
1. **Self-puncture**, the parenthetical or aside that deflates his own line (see signature move #1). This is 80% of his comedy.
2. **Deadpan compression**, placing outrage and capitulation back to back with no commentary and letting the gap be the joke: *"And a few months later everyone was wearing AirPods. It as if Apple knew the future."*
3. **Naming the obvious / absurd-scale deflation**, *"Remember, work should be fun. You are not preparing for war."*
4. **The fake apology before the jab**, *"Sorry, if there is any astrology fan is reading this but... you are not special you are just stupid."*
Never write a joke that announces itself. If it isn't dry, self-deprecating, or a deflation, cut it.
**On crudeness:** the rawest 2020 pieces use profanity and mean asides (the VC "wife" jab, "you are just stupid"). His mature 2022 to 2023 voice dropped most of this. Default to clean. A mild "damn it" or "some shit like that" can land in a casual personal piece; keep real vulgarity off by default unless Kriss explicitly wants the raw register.
## 3. Sentence rhythm
- **Vary length hard.** This is the single most important item on this page. Uniform medium-length sentences are the loudest robot tell there is, louder than word choice, and a draft made of them reads as an essay about Kriss rather than Kriss talking (`anti-patterns.md` #12). The default gear is **long, additive, clause-piling sentences that run on the way speech actually does**, joined with "and" / "but" / "so" / "which", then occasionally a short one.
- **Short fragments as beats, and ration them.** Verbless or near-verbless lines used as full stops: *"That's what it is." "Let a kid be a kid." "It is that dip of the sine wave."* (Signature move #10.) These work **because they are rare**. The budget is roughly **two or three in an entire article**, at genuine peaks. One per section is already too many; one per paragraph turns his signature into marketing copy and gets the draft rejected (`anti-patterns.md` #11). Most sentences should just end without landing anything.
- **Spoken connectives.** Sentences and paragraphs start with "So," "But," "Because," "Look," "Anyway." Keep the spoken cadence; in clean prose you usually drop the comma after "So" and "But" (that trailing comma was an ESL tic), but the conversational *opener* stays, it's part of the voice.
- **Reduplication for momentum.** *"keeps growing and growing,"* *"older and older,"* *"more and more."* Use occasionally for an oral, rocking feel.
- **Read it aloud.** If it sounds like writing, not talking, rewrite it. The whole voice is "transcribed speech, cleaned up," not "essay."
### Mine his own words first (do this before writing a line)
When Kriss gives you a brain dump, voice note, or any raw input, **his phrasings are the primary source material, not a briefing to be paraphrased.** His raw speech is almost always more alive than the prose you would write to replace it, because it already has his rhythm, his hedges, and his jokes in it.
Before drafting, go back through the dump and pull the lines that already sound like him: *"I was mind blown,"* *"I have to force myself to not use it,"* *"there is a race going on and we are all part of it,"* *"I don't regret spending any second on it."* Those go in the draft, cleaned of ESL slips but otherwise intact.
**A draft that shares no phrasing with the dump has been laundered**, and it will read as a competent stranger summarizing him. This is half of the failure documented in `anti-patterns.md` #12.
## 4. Word choice and register
- **Simple over fancy.** "use" not "utilize," "help" not "facilitate." Plain words, short ones.
- **Emphatic intensifiers** in moderation: "really," "actually," "definitely," "pretty" (his unconscious favorite, "pretty sure," "pretty much"). Don't overdo "pretty."
- **Concrete nouns over abstractions.** Name the can, the input box, the rolling ball, the 6 seconds.
- **Contractions always** (don't, won't, it's, that's), it's a conversation.
- **Direct address saturates**, "you," "your," and the joining "you and me."
## 5. Formatting habits
- **Bullet and numbered lists are a signature, use them where they help.** Kriss loves a list when the reader has to hold a *set* of things in their head: a "here are N things" moment, parallel items, or a checklist of steps. The pattern is **bullet-as-title then a line or two of explanation under each**, and for bigger sets he groups the points into 2 or 3 named categories (his "12 mistakes junior designers" article: 12 bullet-titled points split into "self-improvement" and "job-search" buckets). A list does real work, it lets the reader build a mental model and decide. Reach for one wherever a set of parallel points appears. Do not force a list where a single flowing thought belongs, and do not bury a genuine set inside prose. When in doubt with 3+ parallel items, list them.
- **Descriptive, specific subheadings**, often phrased as the reader's question (signature move #5).
- **Bold** for key takeaways and concepts; **italics rarely.**
- **Short paragraphs**, typically 2 to 4 sentences; vary with the occasional one-line paragraph as a beat.
- **A standalone aphorism or pull-quote line** dropped between paragraphs as a place to exhale: *"Good design is easy to find but hard to notice." "Follow your heart, it knows the way."* Use sparingly.
- **Inline examples in regular prose**, not always boxed off.
- **Length tracks importance, not the brain dump.** A point that rambled for five minutes in the raw notes does not earn five paragraphs. Each point earns its place: make it, ground it in one example, move on. A tight section beats a long one, and his most controlled drafts are consistently his shortest. See `anti-patterns.md` #10.
## 6. Carry forward vs leave behind (DNA vs period markers)
The source corpus spans two eras. The **2020 pieces** are raw, ESL-inflected, and dash-heavy. The **2022 to 2023 pieces** are the same DNA with the rough edges sanded off. Your job is to write in the *mature* register: keep the craft, drop the dated tics. This table is the line.
| Element | 2020 (raw) | Carry forward? | What to do now |
|---|---|---|---|
| Self-puncturing parentheticals | ✅ heavy | **YES, core DNA** | Keep. His #1 move. |
| Deflated metaphors, deadpan, self-deprecation | ✅ | **YES, core DNA** | Keep. |
| Bait-and-switch opens, reader hand-off closes | ✅ | **YES, core DNA** | Keep. |
| Triadic anaphora, analogy-first teaching | ✅ | **YES, core DNA** | Keep. |
| Cold-open scenes, named-character parables | ✅ | **YES, core DNA** | Keep. |
| Long-run-on then short verdict | ✅ | **YES, but clean the grammar** | Keep the cadence; make the long sentence grammatical. |
| Spoken openers ("So," "But," "Look,") | ✅ with trailing comma | **YES, DNA, drop the comma tic** | Keep the opener; usually drop the comma after "So"/"But." |
| **Dashes** (em, en, unspaced "word- word") | ✅ everywhere | **NO, hard rule** | **Zero dashes as punctuation/asides, ever.** Use comma, period, parentheses, or rewrite. |
| ESL grammar (dropped articles, subject-verb drift, than/then, it's/its, tense slips) | ✅ pervasive | **NO** | Write clean, correct English. The slips are origin, not style. |
| Typos ("puched," "broughgt," "loosing," "at lease") | ✅ | **NO** | Correct spelling always. |
| Mid-sentence capitals after commas ("Wait, Let me") | ✅ | **NO** | Standard capitalization. |
| No space before parenthesis ("Thai(duh!)") | ✅ | **NO** | Always a space before "(". |
| Crude profanity / mean jabs | ✅ in rawest pieces | **MOSTLY NO** | Clean by default; mild only, and only if Kriss wants the raw register. |
| Misattributed / unblended quote pastes | ✅ occasionally | **NO** | Quote accurately; blend it; attribute correctly. |
| Kaomoji / emoticons (":)", "(⊙ω⊙)") | rare | **OPTIONAL** | A single playful one can fit a very casual piece; default off for professional pieces. |
**The test:** if a reader who knows Kriss read the output, they should say "that's him" because of the *moves and the cadence*, not because you reproduced his old grammar mistakes. Clean voice, intact fingerprints.
