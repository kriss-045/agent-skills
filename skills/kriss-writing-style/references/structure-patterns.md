# Structure Patterns

The seven structures Kriss actually uses, derived from his real articles. These replace generic copywriting frameworks (PAS, Hero's Journey, etc.), they are *his* shapes, with the signals that tell you which one the material wants.

Pick the structure the material is already leaning toward; do not force one. If two fit, choose the one whose signal shows up earliest in the raw idea. When genuinely unsure, default to **The Anecdote-Listicle** (his most common) or **The Single-Insight Reframe** (for one-idea pieces).

## 1. The Anecdote-Listicle
**Shape:** Credibility-by-contrast intro → (optional) split into 2 named buckets → N numbered points, each = principle + "I know this is obvious" disarmer + a personal anecdote or one-line rationale → stop on the last point (no formal conclusion).

**Pick when:** the idea is a set of tips, lessons, mistakes, or rules. Signals: "X mistakes," "X lessons," "things I wish I knew," "what junior designers get wrong."

**Real examples:** "12 mistakes junior designers...", "9 Lessons for first-time Leaders...", "4 things to do to be luckier...".

**What makes his version not generic:** every item is defended with a real story or a crisp reason, and he concedes the obvious ones up front. The anecdote per item is the whole difference between this and a SEO listicle. Use signature moves #11 (humble open), #12 (disarmer), #16 (self-deprecation), #15 (numbers).

## 2. The Question-Ladder (FAQ / Socratic)
**Shape:** A framing question (often the title) → section headers that are each the reader's next question → each body answers it → escalates from neutral framing to the sharpest/shortest line at the end.

**Pick when:** the topic is an explainer or argument the reader has questions about in a natural order. Signals: "how does X work," "why does Y happen," "what should Z be."

**Real examples:** "How not to get scammed by online gurus?" (Who are they? → Are they wrong? → How to tell? → Why it works? → Run.), "How to create better education system?".

**Key move:** the rhetorical-question header (#5). Order the headers as the reader's escalating curiosity. End on the shortest, hardest line (reader hand-off, #9).

## 3. The Named-Character Parable
**Shape:** A pointed question or claim → two (or more) named, job-titled characters who embody the poles → let them act/speak → his verdict → bounce the question back to the reader for the comments.

**Pick when:** the point comes from real people/experience and is essentially about a contrast between two ways of being. Signals: "I worked with someone who...," "two kinds of people," a moral that's easier shown than told.

**Real examples:** "The question you need to ask..." (Sham vs. Bob), "Why you are already winning at life" (Bob, Hussain, Will on the smoke break).

**Key moves:** cold-open scene (#8), specific job titles and tenure for cinematic grounding, ring structure (open and close on the same question), reader hand-off (#9).

## 4. The Bait-and-Switch Essay
**Shape:** Sell a premise/persona/tone played straight for the first beat or the first two-thirds → detonate or reframe it → the reveal recolors everything before it.

**Pick when:** the thesis is best *felt* by first falling for its opposite. Signals: a counterintuitive take, "everyone thinks X but," a reader assumption worth weaponizing.

**Real examples:** "Why it is wrong to plan your dream life" (fake dream-life flex → "Okay, that was a straight up lie"), "Apple Has Done It Again" (admiration → indictment).

**Key moves:** bait-and-switch open (#3), make the fake half specific enough to be believed, reader hand-off (#9). Use at most once per piece, the whole structure IS the move.

## 5. The Stat-Shock Case Breakdown
**Shape:** A shock fact or number (often formatted as a bare equation/ledger, not a sentence) → founder/origin quote as thesis anchor → History → The Strategy → The Lessons (each lesson on an "X is important, but..." hinge) → follow CTA.

**Pick when:** analyzing how a company/person/product won; a business or design teardown. Signals: "how X did Y," "the strategy behind," "what we can learn from."

**Real example:** "How to win like Red Bull?" ($0.09 production cost vs $3.49 retail, laid out as a ledger).

**Key moves:** specific numbers as texture (#15), borrowed authority (#13), bare section-label headers as scaffolding, analogy/concrete detail (the rival's color palette). Collapse the whole piece into one keyword at the end and repeat it.

## 6. The Single-Insight Reframe
**Shape:** Open in the reader's pain or a slippery distinction → reframe the cause → state the key swap in five words → prove with 2 famous examples → widen ("this applies anywhere in life") → close on one sticky metaphor. One idea, restated ~6 ways; the repetition IS the structure.

**Pick when:** the piece turns on a single reframe or distinction. Signals: "the difference between X and Y," "it's not X, it's Y," one core realization.

**Real examples:** "Why most people don't care about your story" ("be different, not unique" → marathon-not-sprint), much of "A bad day is a better future."

**Key moves:** borrowed-authority-restated-flat (#13), analogy-first close (#7), the one concrete image saved for the final beat. Don't pad with new ideas, orbit the one insight.

## 7. The Mood-Arc Pep Talk
**Shape:** Descend into a shared feeling ("we all have those days") → sit in it → reframe via momentum/physics metaphors → climb out to a forward-looking, conditional payoff ("...but only if you start acting on it"). Floating one-line aphorisms between paragraphs.

**Pick when:** the piece is motivational/emotional rather than tactical. Signals: "feeling stuck," "a bad day," encouragement, "you're not alone in this."

**Real examples:** "A bad day is a better future," "Why you are already winning at life" (the reflective half).

**Key moves:** collective "we" open, triadic anaphora as chant (#6), everyday-physical-object metaphors (#7, sine wave, rolling ball, fire/fuel), reader hand-off with a delayed-payoff promise (#9). Warning: this shape is the easiest to let drift into vague abstraction, anchor every emotional beat to a concrete image.

## Titles (article and every section)

Titles are written or finalized **after** the draft exists, from what was actually written, never lifted from the outline. A title is a promise; the body has to keep it.

### The core move: derive, then make them curious

**A title is a derivation of the section, not a restatement of it.** Write the plain description of what the section says, then change ONE word (or make one small tweak) so the reader has a question they need answered. That one word is the entire job.

Kriss's own example, and the standard to hit:

> "My experience as a designer **using** AI for six months" → nobody cares.
> "My experience as a designer **overusing** AI for six months" → what does overusing mean? Did it go badly?

Same sentence. One word swapped. That is the move.

The two failure modes sit on either side of it, and both are real drafts he rejected:

| Failure | Example | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **The stale label** | "The three ceilings I hit" | It just announces the content. Nothing to wonder about, so there is no reason to read on. |
| **The marketing tagline** | "The only designer in the building, somehow beating every engineer in it" | Engineered to sound quotable. Reads as website copy, not as him. See `anti-patterns.md` #11. |
| **The fix (derive + curiosity)** | "I have already outgrown three setups" | Plain first-person register, but "outgrown" and "already" make you ask how. |

When a title is merely accurate, it has failed. Push it until there is a question in it. Average is a miss, not a pass.

### The other rules

1. **Match the content.** The title relates to what the section/article actually says. If the title implies a framing ("the one word that decides"), the body must use and pay off that framing. A title the body doesn't deliver breaks trust on the first line. The curiosity word must be **earned by the body**, never bait. (Bad: an article promising "one word" that never lands the word. See `anti-patterns.md` #8.)
2. **State a belief, not a command.** "What I'm actually telling myself" beats "So pick your word." He shares his stance and leaves the reader room; he doesn't order them. (cardinal rule 9)
3. **Concrete over vague-emotional.** "What this does to your salary" beats "The squeeze on the ceiling" beats "The uncomfortable part: money." Name the real thing; don't slap on a mood label the content doesn't earn (`anti-patterns.md` #5).
4. **A section title relates four ways at once:** to its own content, to the article's overall theme, to where the story has progressed at that point, and to the article title. The draft-QA pass checks all four.
5. **Keep his own title if he gave you one.** When Kriss states a title or a title direction in the brain dump, that is the title. Do not replace it with something you think is sharper. His instinct for the curiosity word is the thing this section is teaching; when he has already supplied it, use it.

### No title directly under a title

**The first section of an article gets no heading.** The article title is already the heading for the opening; stacking a section heading immediately under it is a title after a title, and Kriss reads that as an obvious tell. Let the opening run straight from the article title into prose, and start using section headings at the second section.

*(Numbered items inside a listicle are exempt from the curiosity rule. Those are advice labels and stating the advice plainly is correct, the way "12 mistakes junior designers make" labels each mistake. The curiosity rule governs section headings, not list items.)*

## Within every section (cross-cutting)

Whatever structure you pick, every section obeys these:
- **Open on a hook,** not a meta-transition. The first line of each section is its own first impression. (cardinal rule 10, `anti-patterns.md` #1, #2)
- **Point, then example, then move on.** Make the point, ground it in one concrete instance, don't circle back and re-state it. (`anti-patterns.md` #10)
- **Draw the section's concrete image from the article's ONE metaphor family,** not a fresh unrelated metaphor (signature move #7).

## Story and flow (applies to every structure)

Whatever structure you pick, the piece is a story, not a flat list. Hold these:
- **A shape with a peak.** Setup, rising stakes, a turn or climax, a landing. Graph the intensity beat by beat and it should rise, peak, and release, never a straight line of equal-weight points.
- **Space goes where the tension is.** Elaborate at the peak; move fast through setup and connective tissue. A long section is right only when it IS the peak. A long section on a low-stakes side point drags and flattens the whole arc.
- **The rambliest thread in the notes is usually the one to compress,** not expand. Your uncertainty inflated it; its importance did not. Make the point, give one example, move on.
- **Every beat builds.** No stalling, repeating, or wandering into a sidetrack. The draft-QA "Part D" pass audits all of this with a reader's eye, and the common defect it catches is the weakest section being the longest while the real peak is rushed.

## Metaphor family (chosen internally, before drafting)

Before drafting, pick the single metaphor family for the whole piece (cardinal rule 6) and decide roughly where it lands. This is an **internal** planning step: do NOT surface the chosen theme in the outline or to Kriss at the pause. It only makes sense once he reads the finished article with the metaphor in place, so the draft-QA pass verifies its consistency rather than asking for sign-off up front.

## Output format for an outline (when used by kriss-article-writer)

Title is a throwaway placeholder here; it gets written for real after the draft. Do not list the metaphor family.

```
Structure chosen: [name], [one sentence on why it fits this material]
Working title (placeholder): [rough]
Sections:
1. [Section], [what goes here + which signature move(s) carry it]
2. ...
Core takeaway in one sentence: [what the reader leaves with]
```
