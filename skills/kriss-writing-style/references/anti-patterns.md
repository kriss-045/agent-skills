# Anti-Patterns

The things that make Kriss's writing feel like AI, like a stranger, or like it's forcing him on the reader. Most of these were pulled directly from his feedback on a draft that was locally fine but fell apart as a whole. If any of these show up, the piece reads generic no matter how clean the sentences are.

Each entry: the pattern, a real example of it, why it fails, and what to do instead.

## 1. The hype-tail connector
**Pattern:** state a thought, then bolt on a clause that hypes its importance or announces its relationship to other thoughts.
**Example (banned):** *"This is the second thought that hit me, and it's connected."*
**Why it fails:** the "and it's connected" tail adds nothing and announces structure instead of just writing it. It is a classic AI move, write a sentence, then write an extension of the sentence. It also quietly forces the reader to accept your framing rather than letting the connection land on its own.
**Instead:** state the thought and go straight into it. *"This is the second thought that hit me."* Then the next line is already about the thought. Let the reader feel the connection; don't label it.

## 2. Meta-narrating your own thinking
**Pattern:** narrating the architecture of your thoughts instead of just thinking on the page ("this connects to," "building on the last point," "as I mentioned above," "which brings me to").
**Why it fails:** it treats the reader like they can't track an argument, and it reads like a model stitching sections together. Kriss trusts the reader to see the links.
**Instead:** cut the connective scaffolding. Strong section openings and a sustained metaphor do the connecting for you.

## 3. The cutesy / vague transition
**Pattern:** a transition that sounds clever but doesn't parse on a second read.
**Example (banned):** *"the second thought that fell out of this one."* (Fell out of where? It's vague-clever, not clear.)
**Why it fails:** it makes the reader stop and decode instead of moving forward, and it draws attention to the writing instead of the idea.
**Instead:** plain and clear beats clever-and-murky. If a transition needs decoding, rewrite it straight.

## 4. Negative-energy openings
**Pattern:** opening a section by lowering the energy or apologizing for the topic.
**Example (banned):** *"Now this is the thought I least wanted to have, because it's about money."*
**Why it fails:** money (and most of his topics) matter to people; framing the section as a chore to get through kills the reader's appetite to continue. A hot take or a pessimistic *conclusion* is fine. Opening on negative *energy* is not. The two are different.
**Instead:** stay raw and honest but keep the energy up. The good model from his own writing: *"I'd rather be honest."* Authentic, direct, energy intact. You can deliver bad news with conviction instead of a sigh.

## 5. Fabricated emotional labels
**Pattern:** slapping an emotional label on a section that the content doesn't actually earn.
**Example (banned):** a section titled *"(the uncomfortable part)"* whose body is a hot take, not anything uncomfortable.
**Why it fails:** it's a fake statement. The reader hits the content, feels no discomfort, and clocks that you were manufacturing a feeling. Same family as the unearned title (#8) and the hype-tail (#1).
**Instead:** label it for what it actually is. If it's a hot take, frame it as a hot take. Every label must be paid off by the content under it.

## 6. Forcing the takeaway on the reader
**Pattern:** telling the reader what to conclude or feel, instead of laying out what you believe and letting them decide.
**Why it fails:** it disrespects the reader's intelligence and reads as preachy. Kriss's whole stance is peer-to-peer: here's what I think and do, you decide. (This is also why his closes hand the reader the pen.)
**Instead:** state your belief and your own move ("here's what I'm actually telling myself"), then stop. Give them breathing room. The title *"What I'm actually telling myself"* beats *"So pick your word"* for exactly this reason.

## 7. Scattered, one-off metaphors
**Pattern:** a different, unrelated metaphor in each section (a rental apartment here, a kitchen there, a car next).
**Why it fails:** even when each image is good, the set reads scattered and machine-made, like a generator reaching for whatever fits locally. It's the opposite of a single mind with a single lens.
**Instead:** one metaphor family for the whole piece, extended and called back. See signature move #7. This is the single biggest lever for not sounding like AI.

## 8. The unearned or mismatched title
**Pattern:** a title that comes from the outline or sounds catchy but doesn't match what the section/article actually says.
**Example (banned):** an article titled around "the one word that decides" (tool vs system) whose body never uses or pays off that "word" framing. Also banned: vague-emotional titles like *"The uncomfortable part: money"* when *"What this does to your salary"* says the real thing.
**Why it fails:** the title is a promise. A title the body doesn't deliver breaks trust on the first line.
**Instead:** write titles last, from the finished content. Concrete over vague-emotional. State a belief, not a command. See the title rules in `structure-patterns.md`.

## 9. The generic-AI tells (the "feels like AI" cluster)
These are the subtler markers that made an early draft read like a model even where the sentences were clean. Hunt and kill them:
- **Evenly-weighted paragraphs**, every point given the same measured space regardless of importance.
- **Hedged throat-clearing** openings ("Let's explore," "It's worth noting that," "There are a few things to consider").
- **Symmetrical both-sides balance** ("on one hand... on the other hand") where Kriss would just take a position.
- **The tidy summary close** that restates the three points. Kriss hands off; he doesn't recap.
- **Smooth, frictionless competence** with no opinion, no aside, no risk, no concrete image. Polished and dead.
**Instead:** uneven on purpose (the important point gets the room, the obvious one gets a line), opinions stated, one sustained image, and a hand-off close.

## 10. Padding a thought past what it earns
**Pattern:** giving a thought a lot of article space just because the brain dump rambled about it, or repeating the same point in three slightly different ways.
**Why it fails:** length should track importance, not how long the raw notes went on. Repetition reads as filler and dilutes the strong parts.
**Instead:** each point earns its place. Make the point, give one example, move on. If you've said it once well, don't circle back. A tight section beats a long one. (His shortest-section drafts consistently read as his most controlled.)

## 11. The marketing tagline (every beat engineered to be quotable)
**Pattern:** writing so that every section heading, every paragraph ending, and every reveal lands as a punchy, screenshot-able line. Clever headings, dramatic one-line paragraphs, a zinger button on the end of every list item.
**Examples (all banned, all from a real rejected draft):**
- Heading: *"The only designer in the building, somehow beating every engineer in it"*
- The dramatic reveal: *"It was me. The designer."*
- The setup-then-punch pair: *"Every setup you build has a top speed."* / *"I have done that three times in six months."*
- Item buttons: *"...and it saves you an afternoon and some dignity."* / *"...a summary of blog posts in a trench coat."* / *"...procrastination in a very convincing outfit."*

**Why it fails:** Kriss's reaction, verbatim: *"we are not writing a marketing copy for a website."* When every line is a punchline, none of them are, and the piece reads as showing off rather than talking. It is the same disease as #9 (generic-AI tells), just wearing a personality. The short-verdict snap (signature move #10) is real and it is his, but it works **because it is rare**. Deploying it every third line converts a signature into a tic.

**The calibration:** roughly **two or three short verdict lines in an entire article**, placed at genuine peaks. Not one per section. Not one per paragraph. Most sentences should simply end, without landing anything.

**Instead:** let thoughts run into each other in normal paragraphs. Fold reveals back into the sentence that carries them (*"...roughly ten times taller than the next one down, and it was mine"* rather than *"That one was mine."* on its own line). Let a joke live inside a sentence instead of being the button at the end of it. Save the snap for the one or two moments that have actually earned a full stop.

## 12. The uniform essay register (reportage voice, evenly-sized sentences)
**Pattern:** writing a competent essay *about* Kriss's experience instead of writing as Kriss talking. The tells: every sentence is roughly the same medium length; vague characters ("someone at work") where a real scene belongs; neutral information delivery with no stake; and no self-puncture, no spoken opener, no direct address for the first several paragraphs.
**Example (banned):** *"A few months ago someone at work pulled up everyone's AI usage. I was ten times higher than the person in second place."*
**Kriss's reaction:** *"it feels like a robot saying its story... there's no authenticity, there's no personality to it."* He stopped reading after three paragraphs.

**Why it fails:** his voice is **transcribed speech, cleaned up**, not essay prose. Uniform sentence length is the single loudest robot tell, ahead of word choice. The fix is not to sprinkle signature moves on top of flat prose, which is what produced this failure in the first place.

**The other half of this failure: discarding his own words.** The brain dump is almost always more alive than anything you will write to replace it. When he says *"I was mind blown,"* *"I have to force myself to not use it,"* *"there is a race going on and we are all part of it"*, those phrasings go in the draft. Mining the dump for his actual language is not optional, it is the primary source of authenticity. A draft that shares no phrasing with the dump has been laundered.

**Instead:**
- **Vary sentence length hard.** Long, additive, clause-piling sentences that run on the way speech does, then occasionally a short one.
- **Real scene, real people.** "We are in a meeting and somebody shares their screen" beats "someone at work pulled up." Never a vague actor where a concrete one exists.
- **Spoken openers.** So, But, Look, And, Anyway.
- **Self-puncture early**, inside the first few paragraphs, not saved for later.
- **Keep his phrasings** from the dump wherever they land.

Note that #11 and #12 pull in opposite directions and you have to hold both: do not write flat evenly-sized reportage, and do not overcorrect into a string of punchy taglines. The target between them is plain, additive, spoken prose that occasionally, rarely, lands hard.

## Never-use zone (added 2026-08-15)

Hard-banned constructions, with the reasoning and the wider family they belong to, live in `editing-his-own-drafts.md` under **NEVER-USE ZONE**. The headline one, which Kriss flagged himself in an otherwise-approved draft:

> **`…than X ever was`** — banned. *"that ever was is so bad tale."* It inflates an ordinary comparison into a flourish that carries no information. Delete the comparison and state the thing plainly; do not swap in a different comparative.

Same file also carries the counter-rule that stops over-trimming: **do not strip qualifiers that carry meaning** (`intentionally framed` is not the same claim as `framed`). Cut on meaning, never on word count.
