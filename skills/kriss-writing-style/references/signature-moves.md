# Signature Moves

The recurring craft moves that make a piece sound like Kriss, each pulled from his real articles. Every move lists: **what it is**, an **example** (his real wording, lightly re-punctuated to remove dashes per the no-dash rule; the example shows the *move*, not the punctuation), **why it works**, and **how to deploy it now**. Reproduce the move in clean, dash-free English.

**These moves are seasoning, not the meal.** The load-bearing layer of a Kriss piece is three things, in this order: (1) a real structure from `structure-patterns.md`, (2) ONE sustained metaphor theme (move #7, the single most important item here), and (3) a strong, hooking opening on the article *and every section*. Get those three right first. Then add two or three of the moves below where the material invites them, never a checklist to complete. Stacking every move produces a parody that reads as try-hard; using none produces generic AI prose that could have come from any model. Both are failure. Pick what the material wants, and lean hardest on the sustained metaphor.

## 1. The self-puncturing parenthetical
**Kriss's single most identifiable move.** State something with conviction, then immediately undercut or deflate it inside parentheses.

- *"Just run(or just close that website I guess)."*
- *"At that time it was called Krating Daeng which means red bull in Thai(duh!)."*
- *"and just happiness(okay seriously now just stop dreaming about it and start working hard)"*
- *"(me thinking about my product launch)"*, even admits he's one of the people he's mocking.

**Why:** it punctures his own bravado before the reader can, which reads as honesty and keeps him un-guru. The parenthesis is a stage whisper, the real, casual thought muttered after the official one.

**How now:** after a dramatic or confident line, add a bracketed aside that deflates it to literal reality or admits the smaller truth. Keep it short. (Use parentheses, never dashes, to set it off.)

## 2. The deflated / abandoned metaphor
Start a metaphor, visibly fail at it, and call out the failure instead of deleting it.

- *"Leadership is like a fight between couples. Every couple fights, but it can be… wait… this is not working. Damn it."*

**Why:** it's the funniest, most human version of move #1, the writing thinks out loud and lets you watch it not-work. It signals he refuses polish-for-polish's-sake and trusts you enough to leave the seam in.

**How now:** when a comparison is strained, don't smooth it, let the narrator notice it's strained and bail, in his own words. Use sparingly (once a piece, max).

## 3. The bait-and-switch open
Sell a premise, a persona, or a tone for a beat, then detonate it.

- *"I am a 24 years old writer, who lives in Canada... I am living my dream life..."* → *"Okay, that was a straight up lie."*
- *"At the end of this article, I have a link to free online course on how to stay away from these frauds… just kidding I am not one of them :)"*
- "Apple Has Done It Again" spends two-thirds admiring Apple's minimalism, then reframes the same behavior as cynical marketing.

**Why:** the form becomes the argument. The reader *experiences* the thesis (you got fooled by a highlight reel; you fell for the scam move) before it's stated, so it lands as a demonstration, not a claim.

**How now:** open with the thing you're about to dismantle, played straight, then turn on it. Make the fake half specific enough to be believed.

## 4. Voicing the reader's objection
Say the reader's pushback or excuse out loud, in their voice, then answer it.

- *"I know so many of you will say, can't we just choose only good from both? Look, I am not saying that they come in the package..."*
- *"I know I am telling you to copy someone's work, but trust me, you will learn a lot of small things."*
- *"'wow, this guy is living his dream life at this young age, he must be so talented...'"*, performs the reader's inner monologue back at them.

**Why:** it simulates a live two-way conversation, disarms the objection before it can harden, and proves he's thought about where the reader actually is.

**How now:** find the one thing the reader is about to say "yeah but" to, write it in their words ("I know what you're thinking..."), then take it on directly.

## 5. The rhetorical-question header
Structure sections as the questions the reader is about to ask; the body answers them.

- *"Who are they?"* → *"Are they doing wrong?"* → *"How to find the difference?"* → *"Why their strategy always works?"*
- *"What should be a goal for ideal education system?"* / *"What is the best structure...?"*

**Why:** a question is a low-friction promise. The reader keeps going because each header is literally the thing they were about to wonder. It turns an essay into a guided self-interview.

**How now:** for explainer or argument pieces, draft the headers as the reader's escalating questions, in order. Answer each plainly underneath.

## 6. Triadic anaphora
Three parallel clauses, same opening, usually escalating. His go-to rhythm for a peak.

- *"Creativity cannot be taught, creativity can be promoted. Creativity cannot be measured, creativity can be showcased. Creativity cannot be explained, creativity can be inspired."*
- *"all the successful people go through that, all the artists go through that, all the billionaires go through that."*
- *"If Apple has really cared about nature... If Apple wants a better future... If Apple really wanted to make the world a better place..."*

**Why:** repetition is rhythm you can hear; the rigid parallel makes a fuzzy claim feel axiomatic and earned. The third beat is where the escalation pays off.

**How now:** at the emotional or argumentative peak, build a three-beat parallel. Vary one word per beat; let the third be the biggest. Don't overuse, one per piece.

## 7. Analogy-first teaching, ONE sustained theme (his most important move)
Make a felt abstraction into a thing the reader can physically picture, then *operate* the object. This is the deepest part of his voice and the single thing most missing when his writing feels generic. Two rules, and the first one matters most:

**(a) Pick ONE metaphor family for the whole piece and stay inside it.** Do not invent a fresh, unrelated metaphor per section. A rental apartment in one section, a kitchen in the next, a car in the next, all from different worlds, reads scattered and machine-made even when each image is fine on its own. Choose one domain that genuinely fits the article's subject, then draw *every* analogy in the piece from that one domain, extending it and calling it back as the argument moves. His "Design of Everyday Things" article teaches an entire book through one running object: the humble input box. That is the model. One object or one world, many sections.

**(b) Operate the object, don't just name it.**
- Momentum → *"It is a rolling ball effect. When a ball is still it will take more work to start moving, but once it is moving it is a lot easier to keep it moving."*
- A bad day → *"It is that dip of the sine wave."*
- The unique-vs-relatable point → *"life is a marathon"* (not a 100m sprint).
- Building a passion career → *"think of it as getting a college degree"* (tuition, semesters, graduation).

**Why:** an abstract claim is invisible; a still ball that is hard to start and easy to sustain is something you feel in your arms. And one sustained theme makes the whole piece cohere, it signals a single mind behind the writing. Scattered one-off metaphors signal a generator reaching for whatever fits locally, which is exactly the "it feels like AI" tell.

**How now:** this is a *global* decision, made before drafting and verified after. Look at the whole article, pick the one metaphor family, and assign which beat draws from it where. In each spot, name the image, run it once, extend it with a small instruction ("keep adding fuel", "keep it moving"), move on. Call the same theme back in the close. If no single family can cover the piece, that is a signal the article's focus is too scattered, tighten the structure first.

## 8. The cold-open scene / named-character parable
Drop straight into a moment or a story with named, job-titled characters; let them carry the idea.

- *"'Life is shit, You won't like it Kriss' Bob told me. Wait, Let me go back to the start."*, leads with the punchline, then rewinds.
- *"Let me tell you about two of my colleagues, Sham and Bob. Sham is a maintenance supervisor and Bob is a machine operator."*, abstract claim instantly personified.

**Why:** wisdom spoken by a named co-worker on a smoke break feels overheard and therefore true, in a way the same line in self-help does not. Specificity (job titles, tenure) makes it cinematic.

**How now:** when a point comes from real experience, open on the scene or the person, not the lesson. Give them a name and a concrete role. Drop the most quotable line first, then rewind if useful.

## 9. The reader hand-off close
End by handing the reader the pen, a question back, a blank to fill, a warm sign-off, an action, never a tidy summary.

- *"Apple is a smart company with smart people and… customers. I will leave that blank space for you to fill up."*
- *"You are already winning my friend."*
- Re-asking the exact opening question, then *"Tell me the reason in the response section below."*
- *"That bad day will make you successful not today, not tomorrow, maybe 3 years from now but only if you start acting on it."*

**Why:** it converts a one-way essay into a prompt and leaves responsibility (and the last thought) with the reader. He never closes inward on himself; he points outward at them.

**How now:** kill any "In conclusion" / recap ending. Replace with: bounce the opening question back, leave a deliberate blank, give one concrete next action, or land a warm direct-address line. Stop there.

## 10. The long-run-on then short-verdict snap
A long, breathless, clause-piling sentence, snapped shut by a tiny flat one.

- *"There are so many country that offers free education... if small countries like Finland, Sweden and Norway are doing this. It is definitely possible."*
- *"This job, you come to work everyday at fix time with fix salary, have a weekend with family. That's it"*
- *"...you need to make it easier for them to scan, and showing numbers will get the attention of the recruiter. This is business 101."*

**Why:** the long sentence mimics thinking-aloud momentum; the short one lands like a verbal full stop and asserts by refusing to argue. The contrast *is* the rhythm.

**How now:** let one sentence run long and additive, then follow it with a 2 to 5 word verdict ("That's it." / "It is definitely possible." / "This is business 101."). Clean grammar, but keep the breathless build.

## 11. Credibility-by-contrast (the humble open)
Establish authority through a concrete gap or a real role, never a boast.

- *"I also started as a junior, and while people who were working with me are still in intermediate positions, I was able to hack through my career and am currently a lead UX designer."*
- *"As a hiring manager, I don't want to know what process you used... I want to know why and how you did it."*

**Why:** "hack" frames his rise as a repeatable trick, not talent, which is the implicit promise of the whole piece. The concrete, slightly competitive comparison makes the reader want the same edge.

**How now:** open authority pieces with a specific, measurable contrast (where you started vs where you are; the role that gives you the vantage) instead of credentials-as-flex.

## 12. The "I know this is obvious, but" disarmer
Concede a point's obviousness, then defend why it still matters.

- *"I know this sounds like a very generic tip, but trust me, it helps the most."*
- *"This again sounds like a no-brainer..."*
- *"Without wasting any time, let's get into it. This is something you or your team will eventually get better at, so don't worry about it that much."*

**Why:** it preempts the reader's "I already know this," and the *defense* (a personal anecdote or one-line rationale) is what separates his advice from a generic listicle.

**How now:** when a tip is obvious, say so, then immediately earn it back with a specific story or a crisp reason. Never present an obvious tip as a revelation.

## 13. Borrowed authority, restated flat
Quote a famous line or name-drop a billionaire, then re-state it in his own plain words.

- Margaret Mead: *"you are absolutely unique. Just like everyone else."* → *"The irony is you are unique but so does everyone else."*
- *"Facebook was someone else's idea. But it was Zuckerberg who made it big."*
- Ford: *"If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses."*
- *"Steve Jobs was fired from his own company."*

**Why:** the quote lends borrowed credibility; the flat re-statement makes it his and proves he actually understood it (rather than decorating with it). Household names need zero setup.

**How now:** use a quote or a famous example as a thesis-anchor, then immediately translate it into one plain sentence in his voice. Name the source. Don't let the quote do the work alone.

## 14. Conspiratorial direct address
Lean in and talk to the reader like a friend let in on something.

- *"Let me tell you a little secret about how to achieve that."*
- *"Look, I am not saying..."*
- *"trust me"* (recurring verbal handshake).
- *"Take you and me as an example..."*

**Why:** it collapses the distance between writer and reader and sets the over-coffee register. "you and me" in particular is him leveling with you, not lecturing.

**How now:** use "Look," / "Let me tell you" / "trust me" / "you and me" to mark the moments you most want the reader to lean in. Don't overuse "trust me" (twice a piece, max).

## 15. Specific numbers as texture
Concrete numbers, prices, counts, and durations instead of adjectives.

- *"I had applied to over 150 total jobs, and the one I was hired at was the one I had never applied to."*
- *"You have 6 seconds to convince a stranger..."*
- *"The production cost of Red bull = $0.09 / The retail price of Red bull = $3.49"*
- *"surfing a 25-foot tidal bore for 34 minutes on the Amazon River"*

**Why:** a hard number does rhetorical work the adjective "extreme" or "a lot" can't, it feels researched, vivid, and makes the reader do the math (and feel the gap) themselves.

**How now:** replace vague quantifiers with the real number wherever one exists. When you don't have it, ask Kriss rather than inventing a statistic.

## 16. Self-deprecation as relatability
Confess a flaw mid-point so the fix feels like a friend's workaround, not a guru's prescription.

- *"if you are like me, who reads books and makes notes just to never look at them again"*
- *"if you are like me, who always starts a book and never finishes it"*
- *"I used to spend a lot of time adjusting drop shadows... and I used to give up."*

**Why:** the confession makes him a fellow-struggler one step ahead, not an authority on a podium. The reader trusts the fix more *because* he admitted the flaw.

**How now:** before prescribing a fix, admit the flaw or failure that led you to it. Keep it specific and small (drop shadows, unfinished books), not a humblebrag.
